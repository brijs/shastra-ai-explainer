## Part 2 — Why a Vedic scholar and IIT teacher is here (1:45 – 4:00)

**[01:45]** I will switch now to English, unfortunately.

**[01:52]** I don't know if I genuinely belong on this stage. There used to be this "odd man out" question in our schools. Right? Perhaps I am the odd man out on this stage. But with the blessings of my Gurus I have had the privilege of treading two seemingly orthogonal paths of life. I have had the fortune of studying the Vedas and Shastras, and I have also had the fortune of going to the IITs, studying B.Tech, and now teaching in one of these institutions. So perhaps that is the reason I am here today.

**[02:32]** Everybody asks me this question in today's era of AI and computer science: what is the relevance of our scriptures? Why should you think about it? We have so many people here — given an opportunity, do you send your children to an IIT, or to a Veda *pāṭhaśālā*? This is the reality of today.

**[02:54]** *(V2 — garbled; best-effort)* "…ते पुण्यतमा महाभागा महेशसदृशा ये वेदं \[…\]" — "Blessed and most meritorious are those, equal to Maheśa, who [dedicate their lives to the Veda]." — *they* will dedicate their lives to Vedic study, but not all of us. But today, here I am making a claim: studying the Shastras and Vedas is a **non-negotiable** thing for every person — not only in this Bharatvarsha but everywhere in the world.

**[03:19]** So we have this right — *(V3)*:
> एतद्देशप्रसूतस्य सकाशादग्रजन्मनः ।
> स्वं स्वं चरित्रं शिक्षेरन् पृथिव्यां सर्वमानवाः ॥

I think that time has come today. I will give you a few examples of why. I'm not saying it just for the sake of saying it; I have examples and data points to prove this, and I will spend the next 15 minutes making some of those points.

**[03:42]** When my students at IIT and IISc — JEE toppers — come to know that I have studied Sanskrit and the Shastras and all that, they ask: what is the relevance of all this, and why should we do it? Here is my answer to them.

## Part 3 — Three levels: (1) Intellectual (4:01 – 6:20)

**[04:01]** Shastras are useful to a human being in all walks of life. I will give you three examples. Let's take the **intellectual level** to start with.

Intellectually speaking, if somebody studies the Veda-shastras, their intellectual capability will be transcended to a different level altogether. I keep saying this: give me someone who has studied **Nyāya-śāstra** [logic] for 12 years, and I will make them do quantum physics and mathematics in a matter of one year — and I have done these experiments. This is possible, because of *buddhi-vaiśadya* [clarity/sharpness of intellect] — the kind of intellectual capability one gets by studying the scriptures is unparalleled.

**[04:41]** I will give you another very surprising instance. You all use AI today, right? All of us use AI. Do you know what algorithm goes behind all these AI engines? There is this thing called **Transformers**. A little bit of technical detail — nevertheless I will tell you: the embedding associated with every word (token) is given by a **linear combination of the embeddings of other tokens**.

**[05:07]** So if I say *(V4 — approximate)*: "सर्वेषां शब्दानां \[परस्परान्वित\] स्वार्थे शक्तिः", you will say, "this is some scripture." If I tell you that the embedding of each word is a linear combination of everything else in it, then you will say, "great stuff." It's not that this is a one-off — there are infinite numbers of jumps like this in our scriptures. Trust me when I say this.

**[05:31]** Having said this — the Shastras themselves say that the final and biggest fallacy is **to look for science in the Shastras**. That is one of the mistakes all of us make: whenever some science comes in at some point in time, we try to find it in our Shastras and say, "Look, our Shastras said this!" The Shastras themselves say *(V5 — uncertain wording)*: "…लभ्यमपि नास्य फलं भवति, कृषि-पलाल-कुल्यम् [?]".

If you find science in the Shastras, think of it like finding **weed** [Sanskrit *palāla* literally means straw/chaff] when you are cultivating a crop. You don't cultivate the crop for the sake of the weed; you cultivate the crop for the sake of the crop, and the weed is a byproduct. So-called today's science and materialism is a **byproduct** of the Shastras. So you have to ignore that — that is not the main agenda of the scriptures.

## Part 4 — (2) Socio-physical level: AI as "Deva and Danava" (6:19 – 8:50)

**[06:19]** Then what *is* the agenda? So we transcend the intellectual level and go to the **socio-physical level** and see why we should study the scriptures. The relevance of the scriptures at the socio-physical level is at its peak today. Today we have it in front of us: we are staring at what we call *Deva and Dānava* — something extraordinarily more powerful than us, called **AI** — and we don't know how to deal with it. We have absolutely no idea how to deal with this species called AI, and we are standing there. Who is there to teach us how to deal with this? You go to the scriptures again.

**[07:01]** There is one thing in the Mahabharata *(V6)*:
> श्लोकार्धेन प्रवक्ष्यामि यदुक्तं ग्रन्थकोटिभिः ।
> परोपकारः पुण्याय पापाय परपीडनम् ॥

So: construct all algorithms in such a way that they lead to the betterment of human life. This is what is there in the scriptures. Think of somebody into whose hands all these powerful tools get, and he has no background of Dharma and Shastra — what would happen to human society? We are already seeing what is happening in our geopolitical landscape. It is because there is no *yadṛcchā-santuṣṭi* [contentment with whatever comes]; people are not happy; and everybody is talking about growing — producing more and consuming more — and that we define as the metric of our success.

**[07:51]** If you look at the Shastras, they say that you have to be aware not to go away from *prakṛti* [nature], not to indulge in and overuse *prakṛti*, because the very purpose — the *parama-puruṣārtha* — of the Shastras is **moksha**. The very word says "getting rid of". The Śrīmad Bhāgavata gives this definition of moksha *(V7)*:
> मुक्तिर्हित्वान्यथारूपं स्वरूपेण व्यवस्थितिः ।

Look at the stark difference with today's material world. The ultimate goal of today's material world is to exploit nature and take everything you can from nature. Our scriptures say: give up everything that is not you — *anyathā-rūpaṁ hitvā svarūpeṇa vyavasthitiḥ muktiḥ*. So the Shastra says: the ultimate happiness you are looking for — don't exploit nature to get it; it's **internal**. All your efforts have to be dedicated toward it.

## Part 5 — (3) Metaphysical level: Yajnavalkya, Nachiketa, the Yaksha (8:49 – 13:30)

**[08:49]** Now we come to the third level of the scriptures, the **metaphysical level**. If you look at the Upanishads, there are a number of stories and narratives that talk about this. There is the famous conversation between husband and wife, **Yājñavalkya and Gārgī**\* in the Bṛhadāraṇyaka — you all know the story. He renounces everything and goes to a forest. His wife asks, "Why are you going to the forest?" He gives all his material wealth to the wife, and she asks again, "Why are you going to the forest?" He says, "I have to get happiness; I have to get *amṛtatva* [immortality]." Now the wife asks, "Can't I get *amṛtatva* with wealth?" He responds *(V8a)*:
> अमृतत्वस्य तु नाशास्ति वित्तेन

— don't even hope to have *amṛtatva* with material wealth. Then she asks, "What should I do?" And she responds — this is the most beautiful line I have seen in my life *(V8b)*:
> येनाहं नामृता स्यां किमहं तेन कुर्याम् ॥

"What would I do with that which does not lead to immortality?"

**[10:02]** Think about it for a moment. I got this thought [also] from the kid **Nachiketa**, who appears in the Kathopanishad — again, you know the story. He goes to Yama, and Yama tempts him with all wealth. The boy says *(V9)*:
> अपि सर्वं जीवितमल्पमेव तवैव वाहास्तव नृत्यगीते ॥

Here is what it means. Again, I am a mathematician, so I'll tell you: suppose you take a very, very large number — the largest number you can think of — one followed by a million zeros; you write it down one by one. That number still leads to zero [relative to infinity/eternity]. That is what Nachiketa says: even if you give me 1 billion years of life, it is going to end; whatever is going to end is not something I want. *Yenāmṛtāsyām tenāhaṁ kiṁ kuryām* — what do I do with anything that does not lead me to *amṛtatva*?

**[10:54]** See, we never think about it. I keep telling my students all the time: we do everything in our life assuming that we stay forever.

**[11:06]** Again quoting the Mahabharata — there is the **Yaksha-praśna**, where the Yaksha asks Dharmaraja, "What is the biggest surprise in this universe?" Dharmaraja responds *(V10)*:
> अहन्यहनि भूतानि गच्छन्तीह यमालयम् ।
> शेषाः स्थावरमिच्छन्ति किमाश्चर्यमतः परम् ॥

We see death every moment in this world, but those who live on think they will live on for eternity. What is more surprising than this? So think about it: all of us are going to perish, and all our material possessions are going to perish.

**[11:40]** I am talking about the relevance of the Vedas and Shastras, right? Today, if you go to the Western world, everybody says — there is this one famous quotation. I don't want to take names — the greatest technological leader once said, "**Stay hungry, stay foolish**." You know who I'm talking about? The meaning is that you should never settle. This is what capitalism tells us, right? You should never settle. You should never have *santuṣṭi* [contentment]. Always aim higher, always aim higher. And now — again, I don't take names — those who aim to send rockets to Mars, who aim to go to Mars and build colonies there, are celebrated today.

**[12:18]** I would say that our Vedas and Upanishads give you **more ambition** than his. If you ask Nachiketa whether he wants to go to Mars, the boy says no: "If I go to Mars, will I become *amṛta*?" If you say no, he will say, *amṛtāsyāṁ tenāhaṁ kiṁ kuryām* — why do I have to go to Mars to die? Think about it. So what is the biggest ambition all of us can have? It is the ambition of **being happy for eternity**, and this is what the scriptures call **mumukṣā** [desire for liberation].

**[12:58]** Now paint this picture to students. "Why are you studying for JEE?" "Oh, I want to go to IIT." "Why do you want to go to IIT?" "Oh, I'll get a job." "Why do you want a job?" "To earn." "Why do you earn money?" "To get some other material possession." "Why do you want material possessions?" "To be happy." But all this is going to end in your death! Can you imagine any more ambitious way of projecting life than what the scriptures have done?

## Part 6 — Beyond materialism, and "proof" (13:32 – 15:35)

**[13:32]** Unfortunately, today's science is limiting our thinking to material possessions. We have to transcend beyond materialism — to the very possibility of existence [of something beyond]. Now people will ask me, "Tell me what is the proof of the existence of life beyond materialism, and all that." That's why you have to — I keep quoting this — asking for proof of the existence of *atīndriya padārtha* [things beyond the senses] is like asking a physicist, "Give me the proof of quantum entanglement." As a physicist, what would I say? "Go to class one, study the alphabets, then study maths, then study calculus, then study epsilon-delta calculus [?]. Then after 20 years you will get to know what it is." People don't expect quantum-physical answers in one minute, but they want us to prove the existence of *atīndriya* matter in 5 minutes.

**[14:28]** If anybody is seriously interested, this is the story that I build in front of my students. Thankfully this institution attracts the most intellectual people in our country, so if you give them logical explanations, they take them. And I will tell you — some of my IIT students are studying Shastra with me today. I have convinced them to **study the scriptures for 20 years and then debate**. I don't worry if they end up becoming Chārvākas; at least they will become great Chārvākas, with great intellectual and logical conclusions.

**[15:05]** One of my students told me, "I came to you to learn, and I stayed here with you for 10 years just to prove that what you are saying is wrong." I said, "I am happy." And this guy is coming, and this is the fifth year; I'm teaching him **Nyāya-śāstra**, because he must learn the way to think. Shastras make you learn *how to learn*. "Use all those things and debate with me." And I tell him: if you convince me that all the *atīndriya* things do not exist, both of us will become the biggest materialists together. But use the correct tools.

## Part 7 — Conclusion (15:36 – 16:50)

**[15:36]** So I will conclude. The question is not about why the scriptures are relevant. The question is: **why are we not studying the scriptures despite it being the most relevant thing in the world?** People here know more than me — all the years of all the kinds of things that have happened to this country have put us in this position. But I think it's time. Again *(V11 — uncertain wording)*:
> "महता भाग्येन [?] … ईदृशाः पुरुषाः अद्यापि सन्ति [?]"

— and that is why we are still having people like **Devvrat [Sharma?]** who are here and being felicitated. We should be thankful to **Bhagwat-ji and the Sangh**; it is because of this that it is happening.

**[16:19]** Now I will take leave. I thank the Sangh and the *Ārakṣak* [?] for giving me an opportunity. As I told you, perhaps I don't belong on this stage, but I am very thankful that I was given this opportunity. I will do a *samarpaṇa* (dedication) of the little that I said to my **Gurus and Lord Shri Krishna**:

> **श्रीकृष्णार्पणमस्तु।** *(V12)*

Thank you. Namaste.

# Editor's notes (for you, not part of the speech)

1. **Gārgī vs Maitreyī (9:04):** In the Bṛhadāraṇyaka dialogue, Yājñavalkya's wife is **Maitreyī**, not Gārgī (Gārgī is a different interlocutor in the same Upanishad). The speaker says "Gargi" (also addresses her as "Garga" in the caption, probably *Maitreyi*). I've kept his words and starred it. For the explainer I'd suggest we use "Maitreyī" and mention this gently, or just avoid the name.
2. **"Nyāya-śāstra" ≠ jurisprudence:** The English auto-captions said "jurisprudence"; the Devanagari track and context show he said **Nyāya-śāstra** (Indian logic/epistemology).
3. **"Weed" vs *palāla* (straw):** He says weed; the Sanskrit word seems to be straw/chaff. Same point either way (a byproduct of the crop).
4. **Nachiketa and V8b:** The line *yenāhaṁ nāmṛtā syām…* is Maitreyī's, from the Bṛhadāraṇyaka. The speaker re-uses it to voice Nachiketa's attitude. Nachiketa's own words in Kaṭha are V9 and "na vittena tarpaṇīyo manuṣyaḥ".
5. **Names / terms I couldn't confirm:** "Devvrat [Sharma?]" (16:10), "Ārakṣak" (16:29), "epsilon-delta" (14:12, maybe), "Srinivasa" (0:53).
6. **Opening 0:00–0:06:** The first two lines of the English captions ("qualities of an excellent Veda mantra reader…Panini's teachings") are probably from the emcee/previous clip, not the speaker. I left them out of the cleaned text.
7. **Verification attempt (web search):** V2, V5 and V11 could not be matched to any source. V4's exact wording wasn't found either, but the idea behind it is the *anvitābhidhāna* doctrine of Prābhākara Mīmāṃsā (words convey meaning already linked to the other words in a sentence). These four stay marked low-confidence. V1 is the speaker's own improvised Sanskrit, so there is no source to match.
